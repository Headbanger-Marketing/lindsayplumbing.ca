Plan the request

Asking for unfamiliar terms in a proposal to be explained

A proposal for your Lindsay home may name parts and methods in shorthand, such as an expansion tank, a manifold or a cleanout, that the written scope may not define. If a term is unfamiliar, mark it on the document and list it in your request. The respondent can then give a plain explanation of what the item is, where it sits and why it appears in the work, so you are agreeing to something you understand.

  • Terms you marked

    Underline or list each word in the proposal you cannot explain yourself, and keep the wording exactly as the document prints it.

  • Where each applies

    Note which room, fixture or line each unfamiliar term relates to, so the explanation can be tied to a place in the home.

  • Plain explanations received

    Keep the respondent's written explanation of each term with the proposal, so later questions can be checked against the same document.

Before you send

Photos That Help Before the First Call

A few clear photos help a provider understand the job before the first call. Have them ready for the follow-up.

  • Water heater: the rating plate, the vent pipe where it leaves the tank, and the rental sticker or contract if there is one.
  • Blocked drain: the fixtures that drain slowly or back up, whether it's only the lowest level, and the floor drain or cleanout if it's accessible.
  • Leak: the stain or damp patch, the water meter dial with every tap closed, and any recent renovation nearby.
  • Sump pump: the open pit and the pump inside it, the discharge pipe where it exits, and any backup pump or alarm.
  • Older supply pipe: the line beside the water meter. Copper is orange-brown, galvanized steel is grey with threaded joints, and lead is dull grey and shows bright silver when scratched lightly with a key.
FAQ

Questions About Your Lindsay Request

Does a request listing proposal terms commit me to work on my Lindsay home or to paying for it?

No. Whatever terms it lists, a request leaves coverage, availability, pricing and any visit unconfirmed. Our enquiry team reviews it first, and a provider must then confirm the address, the type of work, their qualifications, the written scope and the schedule. Until those are agreed, nothing is booked and no work has been arranged.

How do I tell the respondent which words in my proposal are unfamiliar?

List each unfamiliar word as it is printed, then add a short note on the line, room or fixture it relates to. A photo of the marked page works well. The respondent can then explain every item in order, and you can compare their wording with the written scope later.

Can I ask for plain explanations before agreeing to work in Lindsay?

Yes. Asking what a term means is a normal part of reviewing a proposal, and a sound written scope should hold up to it. If a respondent cannot or will not explain an item in plain words, treat that as a reason to pause before agreeing to the work, and ask for the answer in writing.

Should a proposal with several unfamiliar terms be sent as one request?

Send it as one request when the terms all come from the same document, and group them by service so each explanation stays attached to the right item. If a second proposal covers unrelated work, such as a tap and a softener, a separate request is clearer to review.

Should explanations given for my Lindsay proposal appear in the written scope?

If an explanation was given only in conversation, ask for it in writing. The scope should use the same words the respondent explained, with a short note beside each term, so the document you agree to has one shared meaning. That helps if a question about a part comes up after the work.

What if an explained term shows the proposal covers more than I expected?

Say so and ask the respondent to separate the item from the rest of the scope, with its purpose stated in plain words. You can then compare the proposal with and without it, and decide whether it belongs. Nothing in a request commits you to a particular part or method.
